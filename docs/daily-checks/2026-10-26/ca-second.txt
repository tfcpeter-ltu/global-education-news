(Abbie Cole/CBC)Social SharingThe number of international students at Memorial University has dropped 45 per cent over the past three years, following a federal clamp down on study permits.The decline echoes universities across Atlantic Canada, where the overall foreign student population is nearly half compared to 2023, according to data from the Association of Atlantic Universities."There certainly was a stark decrease in previous enrolments," said Angie Clarke, Memorial University's interim university registrar.UPEI sees 20% drop in international student enrolment from last yearNova Scotia university enrolment continues to drop, new statistics showIn 2024, the federal government capped the number of study permits nationwide, saying it was responding to the housing crunch and cracking down on some private colleges that took advantage of foreign students. But Canada's Auditor General says the federal changes led to a "sharper decline" in international students than expected — and disproportionately affected smaller provinces.Newfoundland and Labrador was actually supposed to see an increase in approved study permits, an auditor general report said, but instead saw a decline.Clarke says potential students may now believe it's more difficult to come to Canada following the federal changes.With MUN now looking to attract more international students, Clarke says MUN is aggressively recruiting and reducing barriers in the admissions process.At MUN, numbers dropped from 4,889 international students in 2023 to 4,179 students in 2024; 3,199 students in 2025; and 2,682 students in 2026, according to the university.Angie Clarke is Memorial University's interim registrar. While the overall number of international students is dropping, she says there's an uptick in new foreign students this fall. (Abbie Cole/CBC)Atlantic Canada saw 'dramatic' drop Following federal changes, there's been fewer applications from foreign students looking to come to Canada, said the auditor general report. It said Ottawa also approved fewer-than-expected permits."The department did not know why its approval rates were lower than projected," said the AG report.Atlantic Canada saw a "dramatic" decrease in international students after the federal changes, says Ava Czapalay, CEO of the Association of Atlantic Universities. Czapalay said federal visa officers are refusing more visa applications for Atlantic Canada than for other places in Canada, such as Ontario.  She said her association has raised the issue with the federal government. CBC’s Laura Howells reports.Premier wants Ottawa to allow more foreign studentsMeanwhile, Premier Tony Wakeham says Ottawa needs to allow more foreign students in this province, as Newfoundland and Labrador stares down a need for more workers.“The federal government right now has restrictions on the number of foreign students who can come and be educated in our province," Wakeham said during his State of the Province address last week. "That needs to change.”AnnMarie Boudreau, CEO of the St. John's Board of Trade, says MUN needs to work on attracting as many foreign students as it can. She says business leaders are concerned about having enough workers for upcoming provincial projects.Back at MUN, Clarke assures they're working hard on international recruitment. She's cautiously optimistic — pointing to a 14 per cent increase in new international students this September compared to last year. “We are doing everything we can to ensure that that positive start continues right to next September," she said.Rising tuition will deter students, says MUNSUFewer international students is "a loss for Memorial University," said Taqi Yeasir, director of external affairs for MUN's student union (MUNSU).Yeasir, an international student himself, points to rising tuition, saying an annual $22,500 for international undergrads can push people toward higher-ranking Canadian schools. MUN's international tuition remains among the lowest in Atlantic Canada — it's nearly half the tuition at Dalhousie, for example — but is still comparable to other institutions.Yeasir says government also needs better policies for retaining international students, so they join the workforce after graduation.Taqi Yeasir is director of external affairs for MUN's student union. (Abbie Cole/CBC)Hit to MUN's budgetA loss of international students also means a hit to MUN's budget. The university has been grappling with major financial challenges, and has been cutting programs and staff.If international student numbers keep declining, Yeasir predicts there will be more course cuts and job losses."It will also affect the local students as well," he said.Clarke says international recruitment has never been about the money.MUN is dealing with declining enrolment overall, and Clarke says attracting more students in general is a top priority.Ottawa wants more international grad studentsIn a statement, the federal immigration department says it wants to attract skilled international talent, while bringing the overall level of immigration to a more sustainable pace.Ottawa points to efforts to increase the proportion of international graduate students.
