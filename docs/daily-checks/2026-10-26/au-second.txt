'Fed up' public school teachers take to the streets as threat of Term 4 full-day strike looms | Region Canberra

Teachers could see a new pay and conditions offer land on their desks in the next few weeks, but union members have warned it better be good or Term 4 will see another full-day strike.
Australian Education Union ACT members gathered at Dickson Library and on Northbourne Avenue – as well as outside their schools – this morning (21 October) as part of week-long rolling stop-work action.
College teacher Andrew Hirschfeld is in his second year of teaching. He’d heard the narrative of teachers burning out under increasing workloads and pressure, but experiencing it was entirely different.
“It’s non-stop,” he said.
“When it’s one of those things you live every day, it’s hard to break down into words.”
Mr Hirschfeld described the pressure of not taking time off work for illness, because teachers knew there wouldn’t be enough cover, resulting in their classes being ‘flexed ‘.
He also talked about frustrations that teacher concerns weren’t being listened to – either by the Education Directorate or the ACT Board of Senior Secondary Studies (BSSS).
“A lot of problems dealt with every day [at a college level] are with the BSSS,” Mr Hirschfeld said.
“They’re the ones asking us to store tests for years on end, to scan everything into the system.”
He gave his own workplace, Narrabundah College, as an example of where he felt a “disconnect” between what teachers wanted and what the Education Directorate thought they wanted.
Three buildings at the college were knocked down in 2016 and were replaced with demountables.
Construction on a permanent structure as part of the Narrabundah College Modernisation project is underway, but Mr Hirschfeld said it wouldn’t be fit for purpose for the school’s operations.
The building will contain two Hospitality/Vocational Education Training and Food Technology kitchens, despite only 20 students generally choosing to study food tech.
“So that’s two classrooms that are not at all needed,” Mr Hirschfeld said.
“But the [building’s] ceramics room can only fit 12 students, despite it being a very popular class – they all won’t fit inside.
“It’s that classic situation where we call out for the things we need, but we’re not listened to at all.”

High school teacher Lauren Bingley has worked for almost 10 years and said the industrial action was a clear indication that “things have gotten pretty dire” in recent years.
She’s seen teachers take on more and more administrative and communication responsibilities without the resources to do them properly.
“It’s definitely a lot more difficult being a teacher now; there are a lot more roles and responsibilities put on us that would be okay to cover if we had the resources to do more,” she explained.
“But it seems never-ending to do all those roles, before even getting to lesson planning and teaching.”
Ms Bingley especially struggles with having to combine or flex classes, as she’s seen how it affects students’ ambition to learn.
“When you’ve got 100 kids in the library with two teachers basically babysitting, not a lot of teaching is happening,” she said.
“If kids have that multiple times a day or week … there’s definitely a loss of motivation, and a rise in them feeling disconnected from their learning.
“When it happens too often, that’s a really significant chunk of learning that they’ve missed … and it’s hard to get them re-engaged.”
Union members took to the streets as part of rolling stop-work actions to secure teachers better pay and conditions. Photo: John Lomnicki.
Both union representatives felt the Education Directorate and ACT Government were stringing educators and teachers along during this latest enterprise bargaining round.
Mr Hirschfeld questioned why they didn’t receive their first offer until six months after the last agreement had ended.
“We well and truly should have been striking since the very start,” he said.
“Teachers are fed up.”
Ms Bingley said the next deal needed to show serious improvement, not just better pay and conditions for teachers, but also to show the community that their profession was valued.
“We need the resources to run our schools and to give students the learning that they deserve, plus have functional workplaces for our teachers,” she said.
“When we don’t have the resources and teachers, it all falls apart.”

Education Minister Rachel Stephen-Smith told the Legislative Assembly during question time that the government was working to resolve the enterprise bargaining quickly.
A full-day workshop was held with AEU ACT representatives last week to talk about the staffing guarantee claim, and another meeting was held to work through every single clause and claims drafting.
“I believe that the union has been clear that bargaining is now progressing well,” Ms Rachel Stephen-Smith said.
“We are really hoping to get a further offer to the union … in the next couple of weeks so we can get the agreement out to ballot.”
But without a better deal, the foreshadowed Term 4 full-day strikes seem increasingly likely.
“I think most people will be looking at [the latest deal when it arrives] and thinking: am I going to be able to live with this for the next three years?” Mr Hirschfeld said.
“The majority of teachers understand that striking is what we need to be doing right now – we need to be fighting.”
